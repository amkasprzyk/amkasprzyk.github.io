Royal Society, 2023: "Science in the Age of AI"

Pure mathematics epitomises blue skies research. It forms the foundation of much of science and technology; however the applicability of results often comes years -- or even centuries -- later, and is seldom foreseen in the original work. Key examples include modern cryptography, on which our entire online economy depends, which builds on the work of 18th century mathematicians who could never have imagined the digital world; or stochastic gradient descent, key to modern machine learning methods and the success of Deep Neural Networks, which depends on work from the 1950s motivated by a completely different question.

How pure mathematicians work has remained largely unchanged for centuries. It is, generally speaking, a solitary activity: a mental struggle at a chalkboard. This characterisation isn't completely accurate: pure mathematicians have also been pioneers when it comes to embracing computer technology. For example, they have developed Computer Algebra Systems -- described by a Microsoft researcher as the most complicated computer programs ever written -- which allow abstract mathematical calculations to be performed using a computer. More recently software such as Isabelle and, especially, Lean, developed by Microsoft and adopted by the Number Theory community, offer the enticing prospect of formal correctness being determined by computer. However these tools augment and accelerate rather than replace the thinking that a mathematician does.

There is a lot of discussion at the moment about how AI, and LLMs in particular, will soon become capable of replacing research mathematicians. This shows a huge misunderstanding of what pure mathematics is; mathematics it is not largely a matter of symbolic manipulation or formalism -- this is confusing the language we use to attempt to communicate and refine our mental concepts with the act itself. More intangible social motivations such as "beauty" drive mathematicians in exploring the infinite possibilities mathematics allows. In contrast to the sciences, where physical reality naturally constrains and motivates research questions, pure mathematicians are free to explore an unbounded space of uncountably many universes. Selecting interesting questions is the key role of a pure mathematician; names such as Euler, Fermat, and Gauss are revered not solely for the results they proved but for the deep and insightful questions they asked.

I currently see no evidence that AI (or LLMs) can replace the role of a great mathematician in navigating this infinite space of possible questions. What I do see is a new computational tool that has the potential to dramatically accelerate some -- although by no means all -- aspects of mathematical discovery. Computer Algebra Systems allow mathematicians to quickly test conjectures, generate data against which to form new conjectures, and automate symbolic manipulation. This has been phenomenally useful, but it hasn't fundamentally changed what pure mathematics is, or how it is done. AI, at least for the foreseeable future, offers similar assistance. Although this sounds conservative, the possibilities are exciting.

AI tools have the potential to transform the way we approach mathematical research, and we are starting to see hints of this in recent work. There are three main approaches:

1) Highly accurate machine learning models can suggest new conjectures; these conjectures can then be proven using traditional methods. The role of a mathematician in the process is essential: most conjectures, although they may be true, are not interesting, and their proof will do little to advance our understanding of mathematics. But human insight, augmented with ML-driven exploration, can be formidable. This approach is rapidly growing in popularity, and is likely to be common enough that it goes unremarked upon by the end of the decade. 

2) AI models can revolutionise data-driven and computational approaches in pure mathematics. The mathematical computations performed by Computer Algebra Systems are 100% accurate, but often require significant resources (compute time, memory, etc.). ML methods can be orders of magnitude faster and, particularly for mathematically generated data, are often highly accuracy. Blending traditional Computer Algebra Systems with ML tools can make computationally expensive questions accessible, leading to the discovery of new results. Learning how to incorporate tools whose results may not be 100% accurate will be challenging, both theoretically and socially. There are also significant technical hurdles in combining two very different, and extremely specialist, computing environments. I suspect uptake within the community will be slow -- as it has been with the shift towards parallel computing -- in a large part because of the inertia caused by the need to rewrite highly-successful legacy Computer Algebra Systems.

3) Appropriately-designed machine learning models have the potential to guide the process of mathematical proof. This is an incredibly important use-case: just as Computer Algebra Systems have freed mathematicians from many of the tedious computational aspects involved in proof, so AI has the potential to further liberate mathematicians, allowing them to focus on the creative process. At present we lack the ability to extract the necessary information from ML models, however advances in explainable AI might make this possible. It's also conceivable that future LLMs might be able to "sign post" key steps in a proof, highlighting connections with previous work and guiding a mathematician through the process.The computer language Lean will play a huge role here: this pioneering language allows mathematicians to "formalise" a mathematical proof, breaking it down into logical statements that a computer can verify. By combining LLM-driven exploration with Lean-verified confirmation, concerns over rigour and accuracy can be largely side-stepped. This will also have implications for the peer review process, where LLM-generated Lean code offers the potential for unambiguous assessment of mathematical correctness, although it can never address questions of "vision" and "beauty" that motivate mathematicians.

AI will undeniably accelerate the process of mathematical discovery. Over the coming decades its use will become mainstream in much the same way that use of Computer Algebra Systems has shifted over the past 30 years from being niche to being unremarkable. This will not, however, be uniform across the discipline. Areas which have benefitted the most from Computer Algebra Systems stand to benefit the most from AI tools, although the addition of Lean should broaden the scope for impact, and perhaps yield some surprises. Mathematical knowledge and insights are universal, and advances in one area tend to promote insights in adjacent fields. Expect an acceleration in mathematical progress over the coming decade.
